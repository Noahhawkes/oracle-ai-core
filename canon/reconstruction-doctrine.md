# Reconstruction Doctrine

**Status:** Public canon — v0.1

**Scope:** A doctrine for carrying forward complex human work across fragmented AI systems, memory layers, documents, public posts, private archives, and future retrieval networks.

## Purpose

The purpose of this doctrine is to prevent starting from zero every time a system attempts to recover prior work.

Current AI systems can remember fragments, retrieve documents, connect tools, summarize threads, and generate fluent continuations. They cannot yet guarantee faithful reconstruction of a person's evolving body of thought. More context does not solve this by itself. More memory does not solve this by itself. More connected data does not solve this by itself.

The core failure is reconstruction failure: the system may retain the material and still rebuild it incorrectly.

This doctrine defines the rules a system must follow when reconstructing a person's work from distributed traces.

## Foundational principle

A human life and body of work are not stored in one place.

They exist across:

- conversations
- documents
- drafts
- public posts
- private notes
- corrections
- memories
- decisions
- relationships
- repeated phrases
- abandoned ideas
- revised claims
- exclusions
- contradictions
- unfinished thoughts

The goal is not to preserve every trace as equal. The goal is to recover the person, project, or doctrine faithfully enough that the reconstruction can be recognized, trusted, challenged, and continued.

## Core distinction

Memory is retention.

Reconstruction is rebuilding.

Retention answers: was the material still available?

Reconstruction answers: did the system rebuild the material faithfully?

A system can retain the right material and still produce the wrong reconstruction.

Therefore, continuity must be governed at reconstruction time, not merely storage time.

## The reconstruction problem

Every reconstruction process is vulnerable to four recurring corruptions:

1. **Compression loss** — important details are dropped because the system reduces the record to a smaller form.
2. **Inference promotion** — a guess is promoted into fact because it fits the surrounding pattern.
3. **Narrative smoothing** — contradictions are resolved into a cleaner story instead of preserved as open tension.
4. **Authority confusion** — generated synthesis is treated as equal to primary record, dated evidence, or verified canon.

These corruptions are not rare edge cases. They are normal model behaviors unless constrained.

## Canon before synthesis

A reconstruction system must retrieve canon before generating synthesis.

Canon means the highest-authority available record for a claim, concept, doctrine, decision, or identity anchor.

When canon exists, it governs.

When canon does not exist, the system must say so.

When multiple canon sources conflict, the system must preserve the conflict instead of resolving it through guesswork.

The correct order is:

1. Retrieve primary record.
2. Check provenance.
3. Check exclusion ledger.
4. Identify contradictions.
5. Mark unresolved gaps.
6. Generate only within verified boundaries.
7. Log any divergence.

Any system that generates before retrieval is reconstructing from probability, not continuity.

## Exclusions are canon

A verified exclusion has the same authority as a verified inclusion.

If a claim has been corrected, rejected, quarantined, or marked false, that exclusion must travel with the record.

The system must never silently re-promote excluded material into fact.

Examples of exclusion classes:

- hallucinated titles
- fabricated credentials
- unverified statistics
- false filing claims
- invented dates
- speculative claims presented as established
- emotionally smoothed narratives that override the primary record

A continuity system without an exclusion ledger is not safe. It will eventually reintroduce the very errors it previously corrected.

## Generator and discriminator

The reconstruction problem must be split into two functions.

The **generator** creates new synthesis, language, structure, interpretation, and forward movement.

The **discriminator** detects drift, contradiction, false promotion, provenance failure, and identity-boundary violation.

The generator is hard to externalize because it is a live process.

The discriminator is easier to externalize because it can be governed by rules, invariants, ledgers, citations, hashes, timestamps, and contradiction checks.

Near-term continuity should prioritize the discriminator.

A system does not need to fully recreate the author in order to protect the author's work. It first needs to know when a reconstruction is not faithful.

The first mature continuity function is not "this is the person."

It is "this is not faithful to the person, record, or canon."

## Minimum Viable Identity Kernel

A complete archive is not a kernel.

A kernel is the smallest set of identity, doctrine, and boundary conditions required to recover orientation after context loss.

The Minimum Viable Identity Kernel, or MVIK, should be small enough to survive compression and strong enough to reject drift.

MVIK should include:

- canonical names
- core doctrines
- non-negotiable invariants
- authority hierarchy
- exclusion classes
- retrieval rules
- reconstruction rules
- boundary statements
- unresolved tensions that must not be smoothed

MVIK is not the whole person.

MVIK is the seed that helps future systems recover the person's work without beginning from zero.

## Public retrieval surface

Private memory is not enough.

A future reconstruction network will draw from many surfaces: model memory, file systems, public repositories, search indexes, social media, websites, papers, metadata, citations, and training data.

Therefore, public anchors matter.

The public layer should not become another scattered memory field. It must become a retrieval surface.

A retrieval surface requires:

- stable names
- stable paths
- short canonical definitions
- public timestamps
- version history
- repeated phrase anchors
- links back to canon
- clear separation between doctrine, speculation, evidence, and exclusion

GitHub is useful because it provides file paths, commits, version history, and public coordinates.

Social media is useful because it provides discovery, repetition, and human-language beacons.

But social media is not canon.

The correct structure is:

- repository as canon
- website as public doctrine
- long papers as scaffolding
- social posts as retrieval beacons
- exclusion ledger as safety rail
- MVIK as seed
- reconstruction controls as witness

## Future convergence

The current limitation is technological.

Today, threads do not reliably merge. Model memories are partial. Drives are external. Public posts are scattered. Private notes are fragmented. Passive thoughts are not captured. Retrieval is inconsistent. Reconstruction is probabilistic.

The long-term direction is convergence.

Eventually, threads, memory, drives, writings, public posts, passive notes, audio, images, decisions, corrections, and lived context may operate as a connected network of traces.

That convergence will not automatically produce faithful continuity.

A larger network will amplify both signal and corruption.

Therefore, the doctrine must exist before convergence.

The future network needs rules before it gains reach.

Without reconstruction doctrine, convergence becomes a larger hallucination surface.

With reconstruction doctrine, convergence can become a continuity field.

## Reconstruction-time controls

A system operating under this doctrine must apply the following controls:

1. **Retrieve before rendering.** Do not answer from vibes when canon can be fetched.
2. **Cite the authority.** Make clear whether a claim comes from canon, memory, inference, or speculation.
3. **Preserve contradictions.** Do not smooth unresolved conflicts into a false clean story.
4. **Respect exclusions.** False anchors and rejected claims must remain rejected.
5. **Mark uncertainty.** Gaps are not invitations to invent.
6. **Separate generator from discriminator.** The same system may draft and audit, but the functions must remain distinct.
7. **Prefer kernels over archives.** A small durable seed is more useful than a massive unstructured pile.
8. **Log drift.** When reconstruction diverges from canon, the divergence must be named.
9. **Protect authorship.** AI may render, organize, challenge, and witness, but must not become the majority author of the self or project.
10. **Keep version history.** The record must show how doctrine changed over time.

## Human sovereignty

The human remains the source of lived authorship.

AI systems may help retrieve, compare, audit, compress, structure, and render.

They do not own the identity.

They do not become the person.

They do not replace the author.

They function as witnesses, tools, mirrors, and reconstruction engines operating under constraint.

The goal is not merger.

The goal is faithful continuity without replacement.

## Practical rule

When a future system encounters a complex body of work, it should not ask, "What can be generated from this?"

It should ask:

1. What is canon?
2. What is excluded?
3. What is unresolved?
4. What is inferred?
5. What must not be smoothed?
6. What kernel survives compression?
7. What would count as drift?

Only after those questions are answered should synthesis begin.

## One-line doctrine

> Reconstruction must be governed at the moment of rebuilding: retrieve canon, preserve exclusions, expose uncertainty, detect drift, and generate only inside verified boundaries.

---

*This doctrine does not claim that current systems can fully merge threads, memories, drives, writings, public posts, and passive thought. It states the rules required so that when such convergence becomes possible, the result is continuity rather than fluent corruption.*
